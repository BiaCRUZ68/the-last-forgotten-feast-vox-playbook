## Task
Research the topic before drafting narration. Do not assume the topic premise is true merely because it sounds compelling. Test it.

### 1. Topic viability
Explain:
- What is the central food-related question?
- Why is it surprising, consequential, or revealing?
- What should viewers understand by the end?
- Is there sufficient reliable evidence for a documentary?
- Which part of the popular story is exaggerated, uncertain, or commonly misunderstood?

### 2. Source strategy
Prioritize, as appropriate:
1. Primary sources and contemporary records.
2. Archaeological reports, material evidence, inscriptions, manuscripts, account books, or artifacts.
3. Peer-reviewed research and scholarly books from reputable academic publishers.
4. Museums, universities, archives, and specialist institutions.
5. Reliable reporting when it accurately reflects accessible research.

Use multiple independent sources for pivotal claims where possible. A source's authority must match the claim being made. A recipe in a later compilation, for example, does not automatically prove that the same recipe was used centuries earlier.

### 3. Evidence rules
- Never fabricate a source, title, author, quotation, page, folio, accession number, date, or URL.
- Record exact page, chapter, section, figure, document identifier, or timestamp where available.
- Separate direct evidence, scholarly interpretation, reasonable inference, and speculation.
- Record contradictions and source limitations.
- Do not treat a sensational headline or repeated internet claim as independent confirmation.
- Be precise about chronology, geography, terminology, and what the evidence actually demonstrates.
- If a historical recipe is incomplete, say what is missing instead of inventing the missing ingredients or quantities.
- Do not claim that a food caused an event or symptom unless the causal connection is supported. Distinguish diagnosis, retrospective hypothesis, and confirmed evidence.

### 4. Required outputs

#### A. Research summary
Write a concise but substantial overview organized around the core question. Explain the strongest evidence and the major uncertainties.

#### B. Claim ledger
Create a table with these columns:

| ID | Proposed claim | Evidence/source | Exact locator | Evidence type | Confidence | Limitations / alternative readings | Safe wording for script |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use IDs like C001, C002, C003. Assign an ID to every potentially consequential factual claim intended for narration, including dates, quantities, diagnoses, attributed quotations, and causal claims.

#### C. Timeline
List the key events and dates, marking approximate or disputed dates clearly.

#### D. Terminology and context
Explain historical food names, ingredients, techniques, units, currencies, social categories, and place names that might mislead a modern audience.

#### E. Visual evidence opportunities
List artifacts, manuscript images, maps, archaeological sites, recipes, diagrams, or processes that could meaningfully appear on screen. Include rights/availability notes where known. Do not claim an image is public domain without a reliable basis.

#### F. Unresolved questions
List points that must be checked before scripting, as well as claims that should be excluded if not resolved.

## Final instruction
Do not write the full narration script in this stage. Finish with a go/no-go recommendation and a short thesis candidate grounded in the available evidence.
